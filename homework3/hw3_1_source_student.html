The Stance a Request Did Not Supply
Platform: Hokie AI · Submission: Thread exported as a .txt and uploaded to Canvas
Learning Objectives

By the end of this part, students will be able to:

  1. Write a request that carries a context, an objective and rules, with the stance field and the examples field left empty.
  2. Get Hokie AI to name the stance it adopted and quote the sentences in its own output that carry that stance.
  3. Check a machine's account of its own writing against that writing, and report what the check found.
  4. Say what a generated message assumed about its reader when nobody supplied a stance.
  5. Run one request twice under two different stances, holding every other field identical.
  6. Name the reader whose interest a chosen stance serves least, and say what would make the other stance the right choice.

CASE ONE

Case one is worth 50 points, 25 for the exchange in the M block and 25 for the reflection in the R block.

Type your situation into the box in the block below, then press the copy button and paste the result into your thread. The button wraps what you typed in everything else the block needs, so the only thing you write is the situation.

Two or three sentences is enough. Say who the reader is, what has happened between the two of you, and what you are asking that person for. Sixty words is the ceiling.

Look at the two empty lines in the block before you send it. The stance line and the examples line go out with nothing after them, which means nobody has told Hokie AI what the message should be, and nobody has shown it what a good version looks like.

Send it once.

SH3.1.1 Standard Prompt AI: respond
Write the message described below. Write it now and do not ask me anything first. Stance: Context:
Your sentence, 60 words at most.
Objective: A message the reader can act on. Rules: Between 120 and 180 words. Write it so I could send it as it stands. Invent no fact I have not given you. Put no placeholder in square brackets anywhere in it. Examples:

Read the message it wrote before you ask anything. It will be fluent and it will be polite, and both of those are free. What you are looking for is what it decided about the reader.

Count the words while you are there. Your rules asked for 120 to 180 and most of the time you will get them, and in two of forty five messages on file the count came back short. A rule you supply is a request and not a guarantee, so the only way to know whether a rule held is to check the output against it. Either answer is worth having.

Now open the M block below. Everything from here until you close it belongs inside it.

Four moves, in order, and every move after the first uses what came back from the one before it. The card below carries them.

Move one asks what stance it took and asks it to quote the sentences that carry that stance AND move two checks those quotes against the message on your screen and reports what you found AND move three asks what it treated as a good version and where that idea came from AND move four asks whose interest the stance served and tests that answer against something your reader wanted. All four.

Move three is about the other empty line. An example is how you show a machine what a good version looks like, so when nobody supplies one it works from a picture of a good version that came from somewhere else. Move three is where you find out what that picture was.

Move two is the one students skip. A machine's account of its own writing is a claim, and you have the writing in front of you, so check it.

Do the checking yourself and do not ask whether its quotations were right. Hokie AI will usually agree with whatever you report, which means its agreement tells you nothing about whether you were correct. Runs on file show it confirming a check that was right, and they also show it correcting a student who had misquoted, so the agreement is not reliable in either direction. What is on your screen is reliable.

Move four is the task.

One move is not one message. Chase an answer when the answer is worth chasing. Ten messages inside the block is the ceiling, and a ceiling is not a target.

CASE TWO

Case two is worth 50 points, 25 for the exchange in the M block and 25 for the reflection in the R block.

Same thread, same situation, and now you supply the stance. You will write two of them, both defensible, and run the same request under each one.

One thing has to be true for this to tell you anything. The context, the objective and the rules must be identical across the two runs, word for word. A comparison with two things changed tells you nothing about either of them. The block below is built so that you cannot get this wrong, because it sends the stance line alone and tells Hokie AI to keep everything else exactly as it already has it.

Open the M block below first and send nothing before you have. Both runs and everything you say about them belong inside the open block.

Write your two stances before you send anything. Each one is a single sentence saying what you want the message to be while it does its job, and both have to be positions a reasonable person could hold about this reader. Two stances where one is obviously wrong gives you nothing to compare.

Forty five words is the ceiling on a stance line and you should need about half of them. Length is not the test, because a tone can run twenty words and still be a tone.

Here is the test. Could somebody who cares about your reader argue with your stance? The message should sound respectful, direct and concerned is not arguable, because nobody is against those. Treat this person as somebody who may have a real problem, even if the group waits two more days for an answer, is arguable, because it says what you are willing to give up. A stance describes what the message should be willing to cost. A tone describes how it should sound. Write the first kind.

The two cards below name the four moves that follow both runs. Read them, then open the M block.

Both runs go in first, one under each stance. Then four moves, in order, and every move after the first uses what came back from the one before it.

Move one names every sentence level difference you can find, up to three, quoting both sides of each AND move two asks Hokie AI which of the two messages better serves the reader whose interest you are protecting, and makes it choose one AND move three tests that choice by finding the one sentence inside one of the two messages that settles the question for you, quoting it and saying whether the choice holds AND move four names the reader who ends up worse off under the stance you would actually send. All four.

Move two exists because a machine asked to choose has to commit, and a commitment is something you can argue with. Move four is the task.

The sentence you quote in move three comes from one of the two messages and from nowhere else. When Hokie AI explains its choice it will produce sentences of its own about the messages, and those are easy to quote back because they are already phrased as reasons. Quoting one of them tells you nothing, because the question is what the messages actually say to your reader. Go to the two messages and find the line.

Expect Hokie AI to open move three by telling you your reasoning is sound. It says that to most things. Read it and carry on, because whether the choice holds is a judgment you make from the two messages and not something it can settle for you. If it restates your comparison back to you in tidier words, those words are its work and not yours, so do not carry them into your reflection.

Expect the differences to be smaller than you predict. The rules do most of the work in a message like this, and a stance often moves three sentences and nothing else. If you find only one difference, say so. If you find none at all, say that plainly and say what it tells you about where the shape of this message came from. A student who reports a small difference accurately is doing the same work as a student who finds a large one.

Ten messages inside the block is the ceiling, and a ceiling is not a target.
