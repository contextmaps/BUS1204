Anchor Factors at a Level, Finding Where a Plan Starts to Move
Platform: Hokie AI · Submission: Thread exported as a .txt and uploaded to Canvas
Learning Objectives

By the end of this part, students will be able to:

  1. Read a plan and the amount of a resource it uses against the amount available, and say which resources the plan uses up completely.
  2. Say what having an amount left over tells them about whether a resource is deciding this week's plan.
  3. Find the availability below which a plan changes, by changing one number at a time and reading the result.
  4. Report the plan on each side of that value.
  5. Say what happens to a resource the plan was using up completely, once a different resource is lowered past the value where the plan moves.
  6. Say whether a factor is an anchor factor on its own, or only at a particular level, using their own numbers as the evidence.

You will work with a company that plans one week of production, and with a desk that answers questions about that plan. The desk reports numbers. It does not tell you what they mean, and reading them is the work.

In case one you ask the desk what the plan is and how much of each supply it uses. In case two you change one supply, one number at a time, until the plan moves, and find the value where that starts.

The desk answers three questions and nothing else. What the plan is. How much of a named supply the plan uses. What the plan becomes when the amount of one supply is changed, which comes back as the new plan together with how much of all three supplies that new plan uses.

It gives you twenty questions in the whole conversation and counts down after every answer. Both cases together need about ten, so there is room to spend one badly and room to ask something neither case asked for.

Only questions you send to the desk count against the twenty. The ten message ceiling on each block below is a different number, and thinking beside your own notes costs nothing.

Three things the desk will not do, and it is worth knowing before you start, so you do not spend a question arguing with it. It will tell you how much of a supply the plan uses and how much is available, and it will not subtract one from the other. It will not tell you whether a supply matters. It will not tell you what to ask next. All three of those are yours.

CASE ONE

Case one is worth 50 points, 25 for the exchange in the M block and 25 for the reflection in the R block.

You are going to ask the desk four things and then read what came back. Four questions out of your twenty, and the reading afterwards costs nothing.

Four moves, in order. The card below carries them.

Move one asks the desk what the plan is this week and reports the two batch numbers and the profit AND move two asks how much of the cold storage the plan uses AND move three asks the same about the bottling line and then about the coffee extract AND move four puts the three pairs of numbers side by side, says which supplies the plan uses up completely, and says what you expect the odd one out to mean.

Move four is the task. The desk gives you two numbers for each supply, what the plan uses and what is available, and it stops there. Move four is where you do the comparison it refused to do.

Say what you think the comparison means for whether each supply is deciding this week's plan, and say what you would change first to find out whether you are right. You are not expected to be right at this point. Case two changes the coffee extract whatever you predicted here, so a different prediction is not a wrong one.

One move is not one message. Ten messages inside the block is the ceiling, and a ceiling is not a target.

CASE TWO

Case two is worth 50 points, 25 for the exchange in the M block and 25 for the reflection in the R block. Stay in the same conversation.

Case one ended with an expectation about the coffee extract. Case two tests it by changing the number and reading what comes back.

The desk takes one change at a time. When you change the extract, the cold storage stays at one hundred slots and the bottling line stays at eighty hours, so every answer you get is measured against the same week and not against your previous question.

The block below is how you send a change. The box takes a number and nothing else, so type 18 and not a sentence, whatever the label above the box says. Do not walk one drum at a time, from either direction. Halve the gap instead. Try a low number, then a high one, then the middle of whatever is left, and keep halving until two neighbouring numbers give different plans.
