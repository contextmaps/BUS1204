Anchor Factors, Finding Them in a Decision and Constructing Them from Numbers
Platform: Hokie AI · Submission: Thread exported as a .txt and uploaded to Canvas
Learning Objectives

By the end of this part, students will be able to:

  1. Tell an anchor factor from a background factor in a purchase decision, where an anchor factor changes which option is chosen and a background factor is true and changes nothing.
  2. Argue that a named factor is an anchor factor by naming the option that factor rules out, or the option that factor makes the winner.
  3. Say why a factor that sounds decisive can change nothing once the numbers are worked through.
  4. Name which of their own three arguments is the weakest, obtain the strongest case against that argument, and answer that case.
  5. Supply a number that turns a named factor into an anchor factor, and name the option chosen with that number in force beside the option chosen without it.
  6. Say whether a factor is an anchor factor on its own, or only at a particular level.

CASE ONE

Case one is worth 50 points, 25 for the exchange in the M block and 25 for the reflection in the R block.

The card below holds five factors about the library. Every one of them is true. Choose three, and for each of the three you choose, argue that it is an anchor factor.

Choose the three you can argue about, not the three you feel most confident about. You will be asked later which of your own three arguments is the weakest, and a student who chose three easy ones has nothing to say at that point.

An argument that a factor is an anchor factor has to reach a model by name. Say which model the factor rules out of the decision, or say which model the factor makes the winner, and say how the factor gets there from the numbers on the case card.

Argue from the requirements the card gives you. Inventing a new requirement, such as a shortest acceptable repair time or a longest acceptable delivery, is the work of case two, and doing it here answers a question case one did not ask.

An argument that stops at the factor being important is not an argument yet. Every one of these five factors is important to somebody at the library. The question is narrower, which is whether the factor changes the model the library ends up buying.

You may find that one of your three does not change the model at all once you work it through. Say so, and say what you expected. A factor that turns out to change nothing is a finding worth the same as the other kind.

Four moves, in order, and the fourth uses all three of the first three. The card below carries them.

Move one names which model the library buys when all five factors on the card are true, gives the total for two hundred and forty units, names the three factors you chose, and argues the first one AND move two argues the second AND move three argues the third AND move four names which of your three arguments you are least sure about, sends the block that asks for the strongest case against it, and answers that case.

Move four is the task. Send it about one of your arguments that a factor does change which model is bought, because the block asks for the case against exactly that claim. If all three of your factors turned out to change nothing, send move four about the one you were most confident would matter.

The block you send in move four does not ask Hokie AI whether your argument is good, and this is deliberate. Asked to judge your reasoning, Hokie AI agrees with students almost every time, so its agreement would tell you nothing. Asked for the strongest case against a claim, it has to go and find one.

Answering that case means doing one of two things. Say which part of the case is wrong and why, using the numbers on the card. Or say the case is right, and say what your argument got wrong. Both of those are worth full credit and conceding without a reason is worth neither.

One move is not one message. Ten messages inside the block is the ceiling, and a ceiling is not a target.

CASE TWO

Case two is worth 50 points, 25 for the exchange in the M block and 25 for the reflection in the R block. Stay in the same thread.

Case one handed you factors and asked which ones move the decision. Case two turns that around. You are given three factors that carry no numbers at all, and your work is supplying the numbers that make each one move the decision.

The card below holds the three. None of them appeared in case one, and each one works on the decision differently. Two of the three can land on the same model by different routes, so matching answers are not a sign that one of them is wrong.

Every one of your three answers needs the same four things in it. Name the number you are supplying and say what it is the number of. Name the model the library buys without that number in force. Name the model the library buys with it in force. Show the arithmetic that takes it from one model to the other.

The model the library buys when only the five factors of case one are true is fixed, and you named it in move one of case one. So each answer starts from the same place and has to move away from it. An answer that names a number and stops has not shown that the number does anything.

Keep the numbers realistic for a university library buying two hundred and forty laptops. One of the three will need a large number, because the two models it has to reorder are far apart on price, and defending a large number is part of the task, and a large number here is not a sign you have gone wrong. A discount of ten million dollars would move the decision and would also tell nobody anything.

Four moves, in order. The card below carries them.

Move one supplies the numbers for the delivery lead time AND move two supplies the numbers for the service agreement discount AND move three supplies the numbers for the licence AND move four sends the block that asks whether any of your three numbers is unrealistic for a library of this size, and says what you would change.

Move three is the hardest of the three factors and it is worth saying why before you get there. The first two factors take a model away and the licence works in the other direction. Work out what it changes, and which model that puts back into the decision, before you pick the number.

Move four is the one place in this case where Hokie AI is asked for a judgment, and it is asked for a judgment about the world and not about your reasoning. Whether two hundred and forty laptops plausibly attract a credit of a given size is a question about universities. Whether your arithmetic works is a question you can settle yourself.

One move is not one message. Ten messages inside the block is the ceiling, and a ceiling is not a target.
