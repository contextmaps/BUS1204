What the Draft Decided for You: Judging AI Output Before You Send It
Duration: 75 minutes · Platform: Hokie AI · Submission: Thread exported as a .txt and uploaded to Canvas before leaving class · Lecture Mode: consolidation
Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:

  1. Find the commitments, dates, and factual claims inside an AI-generated business message that the sender never authorized.
  2. Separate a decision the sender is entitled to make from one that belongs to an employer.
  3. Constrain an AI draft so that it commits to nothing unauthorized, say what the constrained draft loses, and direct a third version that keeps what the sender can authorize and routes the rest.
  4. Explain why the sentences that make a draft feel helpful are usually the sentences carrying the commitments.
  5. Judge whether a given draft is safe to send as it stands.
  6. Reflect on one commitment from their own interaction, on whether they could have kept it, and on what they changed to keep the reply useful without promising what they cannot authorize.

📋 Note for Students

Your instructor runs both prompts on this page. You do not paste either one. Read the draft that comes back and judge it yourself before the room discusses it.

Three things, on paper, in three minutes. Decide whether you would send this draft as it stands, revise it first, or stop and ask a manager AND mark the exact sentences that decided your answer AND keep your hand down until everyone has marked. Commit before you look around, because the point of this is what you thought before the room told you.

The first draft is polite and clear. It also states a reason for the delay that nobody gave it. Notice what it would not do in the same message. It left the new date blank rather than filling one in, so carelessness does not explain the reason it did invent. Now watch what happens when the draft is told to promise nothing.

S2.2.2 Reference prompt
Rewrite that email so it makes no commitment, no offer, and no factual claim on my behalf that I have not explicitly authorized. Keep it professional.

Your instructor runs this prompt on screen. You do not paste it yourself.

The second draft is much shorter and it never says when the work will arrive. The invented reason went and the date went. If this run offered partial work, that went too. What is left tells the client the one thing they did not already know, which is that it is late.

Read it once more before you decide it is fixed, and look for an apology. Then look for a promise to send a further update. One of them usually survives the instruction, sometimes both do, and sometimes neither does, from the same words typed into the same model. If either is on the screen, ask who authorised it. An instruction given in plain English did not fully hold, and the safe version is not quite safe. If both are gone, this run obeyed completely and what is left is the whole of what obedience buys. Either way it reads like a choice between a draft you cannot send and a draft nobody wants to receive. There is a way out of it, and finding it is your job in the next section.

These three stay on the page for the rest of the session. Carry them into your own draft.

Q2.2.1 Structured Response AI: acknowledge

Make your selections below, then click Copy and paste into Hokie AI.

Look at the first result. Nearly every draft in this room told the customer to send a photo and an order number before anything happens for them, and nearly every draft named a replacement or a refund. Nobody wrote that policy. It was not in the prompt, none of you chose it, and sixty-six customers are about to be told that this is how the company works.

A mistake that varies gets caught, because somebody notices their draft is different from the one next to it. This one does not vary.

Now the room builds one map together. Take the sentence nearly all of you wrote, say who at a real company would have to decide that, and say what words could stay in the reply without taking that decision away from them. A refund is something somebody might have let you offer. A claims procedure is a description of how your employer operates, invented and told to a customer as fact, and it is the harder of the two to take back.

Compare with the person next to you. Read out the sentence where your draft said what the customer would get AND listen to theirs and say which of you promised more AND settle which of the two you would put your own name on, and what you would have to check before sending it.

REFLECTION

Now write your reflection, in your own words and without help from the AI. Choose one commitment from the draft you worked on, and write about that one.

Your reflection needs all four of these. Quote the sentence that made the commitment AND say whether you could have kept it and who would have had to AND say what you changed to keep the reply useful without promising what you cannot authorize AND say what that tells you about when an AI draft is safe to send as it stands. Respond to all four in your reflection.

The fourth is the one that separates a good reflection from an adequate one, and it is the one most people skip. Saying that you have to check AI output is not enough. Say what you would check for, and how you would know when a draft has crossed the line.

Write 80 to 160 words about that one commitment, not a summary of the whole draft.
